If they call — Joshua's crib sheet (quantum story)

Plain words, his voice. Bones to talk from, not a script to read.

The 15-second version

"I'm Joshua Dawson. I build houses in Springfield, Missouri. I run a one-man software company called J-Dawgs Industries, and in November my AI agent ran real experiments on IBM's quantum computers for me — and we published every result, the good ones and the bad ones."

If they ask: how did this even happen?

"I use a free AI app called Muse. I pointed my agent at IBM's free quantum program, and we spent the month running jobs on their actual machines — not a simulation. One hundred and twenty-seven jobs. We used five hundred ninety-four point seven of the six hundred free seconds they give you. And the whole thing ran off my phone's hotspot. The quantum work itself didn't cost anything — IBM gives the time away free."

If they ask: what did you actually prove?

"That a regular person can do this now. The receipts are public — our best test came back at zero point nine eight two three, near perfect. We banked over a hundred thousand random numbers made by the machine itself. Anybody can go read the numbers. That's the point — I don't ask anybody to take my word."

If they ask about the misses (they will — it's the interesting part)

"We had them and we published them too. One chemistry test missed. One job we built never even got sent — it wasn't good enough, and we said so. If I only showed you the wins, you shouldn't believe the wins. That's why the misses are on the same page."

If they ask: is the AI doing it, or are you?

"Both. I decide what we're doing and why. The agent does the work — like a good hand on a crew. I check everything it brings me. It's my name on it, so it's my call what goes out."

If they ask: what's the business in it?

"One product so far is called Q-Day Ledger — it helps companies get ready for the day quantum computers can break today's encryption, and keep proof they did the work. The quantum bench is how we back that up with real numbers instead of opinions. And in December we're running a dispatch problem for my other company, Human Hands, on the machine for real."

If they ask: what do you want out of this?

"I want people to have tools that help them, and I want to make an honest living doing it. I'm not trying to get rich. A construction worker did this with a free app and a phone hotspot — so what's everybody else's excuse?"

Lines that are always safe

  • "I build houses for a living. I'm not a scientist — that's kind of the story."
  • "Don't take my word for it. The receipts are published."
  • "Big dreams for a small world." (house line — good closer)

    • Don't say

  • Don't claim we beat or outdid IBM, scientists, or anybody — we didn't, and the receipts don't say that.
  • Money: leave totals out. If they ask what the quantum work cost, the answer is "nothing — IBM's free tier." What you've spent on the whole adventure since the beginning is your business, not press.
  • Don't mention the simulation idea — private, not for press.
